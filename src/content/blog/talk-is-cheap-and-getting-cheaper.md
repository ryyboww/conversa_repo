Language is among the principal institutions through which society holds itself together. Through words, people make promises, acknowledge obligations, communicate intentions, resolve disagreements, and establish expectations concerning future conduct. Language allows individuals who possess different experiences, interests, and perspectives to coordinate their lives within a shared social world. Its importance therefore extends beyond expression or the transmission of information. Trusted language produces connection. It gives people reason to rely upon one another and helps sustain the relationships upon which families, communities, institutions, and societies depend.

In this sense, language functions as social glue. It binds people across time because words allow obligations formed in one moment to remain meaningful in another. A promise enables someone to act in anticipation of another person’s future conduct. An explanation can restore intelligibility when an action creates uncertainty. An apology may acknowledge injury and begin repairing a damaged relationship. Public statements can clarify institutional responsibilities and provide standards against which authority may later be judged.

The strength of these bonds, however, depends upon trust.

Words retain social value when people believe some meaningful relationship exists between what is said, what is intended, and what will follow. Trust makes communication usable. It allows people to accept statements, commitments, and explanations without independently investigating every claim or continuously preparing for deception. As Niklas Luhmann argued, trust helps people manage the complexity and uncertainty accompanying social life. Without it, the range of conduct upon which individuals can confidently rely becomes considerably narrower (Luhmann, 1979).

When trust diminishes, communication becomes more burdensome. Every promise requires documentation. Every explanation invites suspicion. Every commitment demands verification. Every inconsistency becomes possible evidence of manipulation. People may continue to speak, but their words possess less capacity to settle uncertainty or coordinate action.

This is why declining trust in what people say presents a problem extending beyond individual dishonesty. When distrust becomes widespread, language itself begins to lose value as a social instrument. People may continue talking, posting, explaining, promising, apologizing, and issuing public statements, yet their words carry less weight. Communication expands in volume while declining in credibility.

Under these conditions, the familiar expression “talk is cheap” acquires greater social significance. Talk becomes cheap not simply because words are easy to produce, but because fewer obligations appear to accompany their use. Statements can be revised, deleted, reframed, denied, or displaced by new statements before responsibility becomes settled. Apologies may serve reputation management rather than reconciliation. Public commitments may generate favorable impressions without producing corresponding institutional change.

As the relationship between language and obligation weakens, so does the bonding capacity of communication. Words become less capable of connecting speaker and listener because the listener can no longer assume the speaker regards language as binding. What deteriorates is not merely confidence in one statement. The underlying relationship permitting individuals to orient themselves toward one another also begins to weaken.

This problem has implications for the organization of society. Émile Durkheim’s account of social solidarity directs attention to the moral bonds through which differentiated individuals remain connected within collective life. Social order depends not only upon formal arrangements or exchanges, but also upon forms of mutual dependence and shared obligation capable of binding people to one another (Durkheim, 1984).

Jürgen Habermas’s theory of communicative action similarly treats language as central to the achievement of mutual understanding, the coordination of conduct, and the reproduction of social relationships. Communication, within this framework, is not secondary to social order. It is among the processes through which social integration becomes possible (Habermas, 1984, 1987).

When communication loses credibility, people become less able to develop shared understandings across differences. They may retreat into smaller circles of trust, accepting statements primarily from people who share their identities, experiences, political commitments, or social positions. Language no longer serves as a bridge across social boundaries. It becomes a marker of affiliation, a defensive instrument, or a weapon used to discredit opposing groups.

Society may remain saturated with speech while losing the communicative bonds required for collective life.

Technological change may now be accelerating this process in historically unprecedented ways. Digital systems can produce persuasive arguments, personal correspondence, institutional reports, official notices, public statements, and apparent expressions of concern at extraordinary speed. These capabilities provide meaningful benefits, but they also reduce many of the costs once associated with producing polished and credible language.

Eloquence no longer reliably signals knowledge. Confidence does not establish competence. Personal tone does not confirm personal investment. Apparent sincerity may reveal little about who produced the words, why they were generated, or whether anyone accepts responsibility for their consequences.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology has identified risks generative technologies pose to the transparency, credibility, and trustworthiness of digital information. Its work on synthetic content emphasizes the importance of provenance: people increasingly need ways to determine where content originated, how it was produced, and whether it has been altered (National Institute of Standards and Technology [NIST], 2024).

The central issue is not whether technologically assisted language should be considered inherently false or inauthentic. Human beings have always used tools, conventions, assistants, templates, and institutional processes to produce language. The more consequential question concerns whether people can continue relying upon words when authorship, intention, knowledge, and responsibility become increasingly difficult to locate.

Words bind people partly because someone stands behind them. A promise possesses social force because someone can be called upon to honor it. An explanation matters because someone accepts responsibility for its adequacy. An institutional statement creates expectations because the institution can be judged according to the relationship between its language and its conduct.

When words appear without a clearly accountable speaker, the connection between communication and obligation becomes uncertain.

This concern acquires greater importance as social institutions rapidly adopt technological systems whose operations may not be fully understood by the people using them. Schools, courts, workplaces, government agencies, corporations, health systems, and other organizations increasingly rely upon automated processes to classify information, evaluate conduct, generate documents, communicate decisions, and interact with the public.

Institutional authority depends upon language. Policies establish expectations. Reports classify events. Judicial opinions explain legal conclusions. Employment decisions communicate organizational judgment. Government notices tell members of the public what has occurred, what is required, and what options remain available. Institutional language is therefore inseparable from institutional power.

John Searle’s work on social reality helps illuminate this relationship. Many institutional arrangements exist and retain force because people collectively recognize the meanings attached to words, rules, statuses, and declarations. Language does not merely describe institutions from the outside; it participates in creating and maintaining institutional reality (Searle, 1995).

A judicial ruling, school policy, disciplinary notice, public report, or government statement consequently performs social work. It identifies which facts matter, whose experiences receive recognition, which forms of conduct become legitimate, and how responsibility will be distributed. Institutional language tells people how authority understands them and where they stand in relation to collective rules.

When this language becomes formulaic, evasive, automated, or inconsistent with observable conduct, distrust may extend beyond the particular communication. It may attach to the institution itself.

Institutional statements concerning fairness, transparency, inclusion, accountability, or public service retain significance only when people can identify some meaningful relationship between those commitments and institutional practice. When official language repeatedly fails to correspond with experience, even truthful statements may lose persuasive force. The broader communicative environment has already taught people not to rely upon them.

This relationship between communication and institutional legitimacy is especially important in legal and governmental settings. Tom Tyler’s research demonstrates that people’s willingness to accept legal authority is strongly connected to whether they regard institutions and procedures as legitimate, fair, and worthy of respect. Institutional compliance cannot be reduced to fear of punishment; it also depends upon how people interpret their treatment and their relationship to authority (Tyler, 2006).

Language plays an essential role in this process. Institutions explain procedures through language, justify decisions through language, recognize the experiences of affected people through language, and communicate whether public concerns have been taken seriously. When these explanations appear manipulative, inaccessible, or detached from actual reasoning, institutional trust becomes more difficult to sustain.

The deterioration of trusted language therefore produces consequences at multiple levels.

Interpersonally, it weakens promises, explanations, apologies, and commitments.

Socially, it reduces the capacity of people to coordinate conduct and maintain relationships across difference.

Institutionally, it weakens the language through which authority explains itself, communicates obligations, and seeks public legitimacy.

Societally, it erodes one of the principal bonds enabling people to inhabit a shared world.

As trust declines, societies often respond by producing additional language: more policies, disclosures, contracts, disclaimers, verification procedures, monitoring systems, and formal documentation. These measures may be necessary, but they also reveal how much social coordination has been lost. Where a trusted statement once sufficed, an elaborate administrative structure may now be required.

The paradox is difficult to ignore. Distrust produces more communication, yet much of this communication exists because words alone are no longer trusted.

Restoring the value of language will therefore require more than encouraging politeness, improving public messaging, or teaching people to communicate more effectively. The problem is not simply how language is delivered. It concerns the social and institutional conditions under which words become worthy of reliance.

Speakers must remain answerable for what they say. Promises must retain consequences. Explanations must clarify rather than conceal. Apologies must acknowledge obligations arising from injury. Institutional commitments must correspond with institutional conduct. Technological systems must not obscure where judgment, authorship, and responsibility reside.

Trust and language sustain one another. Trust gives words social value, while credible language provides a foundation upon which trust can develop. Together, they create bonds allowing people to cooperate without constant surveillance, verification, or force.

When those bonds weaken, society does not simply become less polite or less communicative. It becomes less capable of holding itself together.

Talk has always been comparatively inexpensive to produce. What may now be getting cheaper is its capacity to bind, reassure, clarify, and create obligation. As words become easier to generate and harder to evaluate, we must become more attentive to the people, institutions, and technologies determining whether language remains credible.

The future of communication will not depend solely upon how much we are capable of saying. It will depend upon whether our words continue to provide others with sufficient reason to believe, respond, cooperate, and remain connected.
